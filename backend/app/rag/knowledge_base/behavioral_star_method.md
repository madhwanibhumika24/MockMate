# Behavioral Interviews and the STAR Method

## What STAR stands for
STAR is a structure for answering behavioral questions: Situation (brief
context -- what was going on), Task (what specifically was your
responsibility), Action (what you actually did, in detail -- this should be
the longest part of the answer), and Result (what happened, ideally with a
concrete outcome or number). The most common weakness in real answers is
spending too long on Situation and too little on Action -- a good coaching
note points this out directly rather than just asking another question.

## Common behavioral themes to probe
Strong behavioral interviews rotate through a small set of themes rather
than asking the same question repeatedly: handling conflict with a
teammate or manager, a time a project failed or nearly failed, taking
initiative beyond what was asked, receiving and acting on difficult
feedback, and prioritizing under pressure with limited time or resources.
Each theme reveals something different about how a candidate works with
other people and handles adversity, which a single "tell me about yourself"
question can't surface.

## Signs of a weak vs. strong STAR answer
A weak answer stays vague ("we worked together and it went well"), uses
"we" throughout without ever clarifying the candidate's own specific
contribution, or skips the Result entirely. A strong answer names a
specific, personal action the candidate took, is honest about tension or
difficulty along the way rather than presenting a conflict-free story, and
ends with a measurable or at least clearly observable outcome. Feedback on
behavioral answers should point out specifically which of these elements
was missing, not just say "be more specific."

## Follow-up questions that reveal authenticity
The best behavioral interviewers don't just accept the first answer -- they
ask a natural follow-up like "what would you do differently now?" or "how
did the other person react?" A candidate who has a real story can go deeper
naturally; one who is describing a rehearsed or fabricated story often
struggles with unexpected follow-ups. This is a useful signal to build into
an AI interviewer's later turns in the same stage, not just its first
question.
