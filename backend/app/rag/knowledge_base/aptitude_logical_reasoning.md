# Logical Reasoning Formula Reference

## Blood Relations
Read the statement one relation at a time and sketch a small family tree as you go, rather than holding it all in your head
"Mother's brother" and "father's brother" both mean uncle; "father's or mother's sister" means aunt
"Son of my mother's only sister" is my cousin
"Husband of my daughter" is my son-in-law; "wife of my son" is my daughter-in-law
When a chain of relations is given, resolve it two people at a time before combining the whole chain

Worked example: Pointing to a photograph, a man says, "She is the daughter of my grandfather's only son." How is the woman related to the man?
The man's grandfather's only son is the man's own father (assuming no other son). So the daughter of the man's father is the man's sister.

## Syllogisms
"All A are B" plus "All B are C" allows the conclusion "All A are C"
"All A are B" plus "No B is C" allows the conclusion "No A is C"
"Some A are B" does not allow "Some A are not B" to be concluded, and vice versa -- both could independently be true
A conclusion is valid only if it strictly follows from the given statements, even if it also happens to be true in real life
Draw a simple Venn diagram for the statement combination whenever the wording feels ambiguous, rather than guessing

Worked example: Statements: "All pens are books. Some books are pencils." Conclusion: "Some pencils are pens." Is this valid?
Not valid -- "books" is the common middle term, but nothing links "pens" and "pencils" directly, since "some books are pencils" doesn't say which books.

## Coding-Decoding
For letter-shift codes, work out how many places each letter has moved in the alphabet, for example CAT to DBU is a +1 shift on every letter
For number codes, check whether each letter is replaced by its alphabet position (A=1, B=2, and so on) or some function of it
For word codes, compare the coded word to the original letter by letter to spot the pattern, rather than guessing a rule upfront
Always verify a pattern against a second example in the question before applying it, since more than one rule can fit a single example

Worked example: If CAT is coded as DBU, how is DOG coded using the same rule?
Each letter shifts forward by 1: C to D, A to B, T to U. Applying the same shift to D, O, G gives E, P, H, so DOG is coded as EPH.

## Seating Arrangement
Start by drawing the seats, a line or a circle, and place the most specific clue first
For a circular arrangement facing the center, left and right are swapped compared to facing outward, so fix a facing direction before anything else
Process clues that fix a definite position before clues that only state relative order
When a clue allows two possible arrangements, keep both open and use later clues to eliminate one

Worked example: Five friends sit in a row. A is immediately to the right of B. C is at one end. D is between A and E. Where can B sit?
Since C occupies one end and D sits between A and E, fitting the A-D-E block and the B-A adjacency into the remaining seats places B in the second position from the open end.

## Number Series
Check the simplest patterns first: a constant difference, a constant ratio, or alternating differences
Common patterns include squares, cubes, and prime numbers, so check these before assuming a plain arithmetic or geometric pattern
For an alternating series, split it into two separate sub-series, the odd positions and the even positions, and look for a pattern in each
If no straightforward pattern fits, check the differences between consecutive terms as a new series of their own

Worked example: Find the next term: 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ?
The differences are 4, 6, 8, 10, increasing by 2 each time, so the next difference is 12: 30 + 12 = 42.
